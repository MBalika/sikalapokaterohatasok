External effects on shallow foundations

We can calculate the settlement caused by static load well. But a foundation is also subject to other, often unexpected effects: the ground beneath it vibrates, the earth moves, a cavity opens below it, water washes it out, or frost lifts it – and expansive clay moves it in a similar way. These are not to be calculated but recognised and prevented.

CH0Overview

What acts on the foundation?

Of the factors that cause building movement and settlement, it is the deformation due to static load that we can calculate relatively accurately. Many other – often unexpected – effects can also cause settlement: against these we protect ourselves not by calculation but by recognition, detailing and preventive measures.

Apart from static loads, shallow foundations can be subject to four further effects. Each of them can cause settlement or deformation, and each has a different source: vibration through rearrangement of the soil grains, a cavity through movement of the ground, water through carrying away grains, and frost through the growth of ice lenses. Of the dynamic effects, the earthquake is the least predictable and causes the most damage – so it has its own channel.

Expansive (swelling and shrinking) clay also belongs here. High-plasticity clay swells or shrinks as its water content changes, and so – like frost action – it lifts and lowers a lightly loaded building standing on it in a seasonal cycle. This effect is covered in detail on the Foundations on unfavourable ground page; here we only show its place among the other effects.

These are prevented, not calculated

Unlike static settlement, the magnitude of movement caused by vibration, cavities, erosion and frost can rarely be predicted reliably. The designer’s task is to recognise the hazard already at the design stage: through ground investigation, by mapping the surroundings (neighbouring construction, cellars, riverbank, groundwater), and with detailing that keeps the effect away from the foundation.

CH1vibration · resonance · compaction

Dynamic effects

Industrial buildings in particular may carry, in addition to static loads, dynamic loads that change suddenly or continuously over time. This effect can also spread to the foundations and the subsoil – machine foundations, for example, are dynamically loaded foundations. The dynamic behaviour of soils is covered in detail in the MSc programme; here we summarise the most important points.

01 · sourcesWhere does the vibration come from?

The sources of dynamic effects on structures: blasting, piling (and sheet piling), traffic, earthquakes and wind gusts. What they have in common is that they typically act for a short time, cyclically – not as a permanent load. Their frequency, duration and magnitude, however, differ greatly: an earthquake is a slow, large-amplitude movement, vibratory pile driving is a steady, rapid vibration, and blasting is a single short shock.

02 · sensitivityWhich soil is sensitive to vibration?

Fine-grained, steeply (uniformly) graded soils are particularly sensitive – especially when saturated with water. In loose sand made of uniform grains, vibration makes the grains rearrange, their skeleton collapses into a denser arrangement and the surface settles. In saturated soil the pore water cannot escape immediately: the water pressure rises, and the pressure between the grains, and with it the shear strength, drops. Cohesive soils are protected by cohesion, well-graded coarse soils by the “interlocking” of the grains – these are less sensitive.

03 · resonanceNatural frequency and excitation

The soil mass beneath a structure – together with the foundation and the structure – has a natural frequency at which it responds to the smallest excitation with the largest amplitude. If the excitation frequency (for example the speed of a machine or the frequency of a vibrator) falls close to it, within the resonance range, resonance develops, and this can lead to sudden, collapse-like settlement. The practical conclusion is simple: the natural frequency of the soil mass must not coincide with the excitation frequency.

04 · damageTwo kinds of damage

01 · structure

Direct damage to the superstructure

Vibration directly damages the structural elements of the building: cracks appear in the masonry, the plaster and at floor connections, and chimneys and cornices work loose. The limiting values therefore refer to the vibration velocity measured on the foundation and on the top floor.

02 · soil

Damage from soil compaction

The soil beneath the structure compacts and deforms – this causes settlement, often differential settlement. On loose sand this is the more dangerous: even a small vibration velocity can cause permanent settlement, even if it is not yet directly dangerous to the structure.

05 · zone of influenceHow far does the vibration reach?

The zone of influence of the vibration source must always be assessed: how far the vibrations can travel through the subsoil. Often the construction itself generates the vibration (piling, sheet piling), and this also affects neighbouring structures. Such vibrations typically travel no more than 20–30 m from the source. Vibration attenuates with distance for two reasons: the wave spreads over an ever larger area (geometric damping), and friction in the soil absorbs its energy (material damping). The permissible vibration velocity at a neighbouring building is set by a standard – the widely used German DIN 4150-3, for example, by type of building and vibration frequency.

06 · compactionSettlement crater around the pile

In loose and medium-dense sand, pile driving itself compacts the soil. Compaction occurs in a zone around the pile with a radius of roughly three pile diameters, but at the surface a much wider, funnel-shaped settlement crater forms – its radius is roughly 3·D + L/2. This is why it is considered dangerous if a neighbouring foundation is closer to a driven pile than one pile length.

07 · machine foundationsMachine foundations

Machine foundations (for turbines, compressors, presses, pumps) are dynamically loaded foundations. Two things must be checked in their design: the operating frequency of the machine must not fall close to the natural frequency of the system consisting of the machine, the foundation and the soil, and the vibration amplitude must not exceed the value permitted for the machine and its surroundings. The machine foundation is separated from the rest of the building by a movement joint, so that the vibration does not pass to the floor and the walls.

How much vibration can be felt?

People are much more sensitive to vibration than buildings: continuous vibration is clearly perceptible at around 1 mm/s, and 2.5 mm/s is strong. In buildings next to busy roads, traffic vibration rarely reaches 2 mm/s; at 5 mm/s it is already disturbing. The 5 mm/s limit for residential buildings is therefore not the limit of discomfort but of damage.

CH2focus · intensity · EC8

Earthquakes

Of the dynamic effects, earthquakes damage structures the most, and they are also the least predictable. An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the Earth’s surface, triggered by a rupture, a sudden displacement caused by stress accumulated in the rocks of the solid crust. Its strength can range from vibration detectable only by instruments to shaking that destroys cities and even changes the landscape.

01 · originFrom the focus to the surface

Our Earth is an active planet: material flows in its hot interior move the huge rock plates that make up the crust, which shift relative to each other by a few centimetres a year. Stress accumulates along their boundaries because of this movement, and when it exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock suddenly slips along an existing or newly formed fracture plane (fault). The foci of more than 90 % of earthquakes lie at plate boundaries – these, however, are not lines but deformation zones often several hundred kilometres wide. Smaller stresses can also accumulate within the plates, so moderate or smaller earthquakes can occur far from the boundaries too.

The point deep in the Earth where the rupture of the rock begins is called the hypocentre or focus. The point on the surface above the focus is the epicentre; the distance between the two is the focal depth. This can range from a few kilometres to as much as 600–700 km, but the vast majority of earthquakes are at most 30 km deep. The released energy generates elastic waves: the faster, compressional P-waves arrive first, followed by the slower, shear S-waves, and finally the surface waves – these are recorded by seismographs even at great distances.

circum-Pacific belt≈ 81%

Most of the largest earthquakes occur in the belt around the Pacific Ocean, at colliding plate boundaries.

Alpine–Himalayan belt≈ 17%

It stretches from the Mediterranean region through Turkey, Central Asia and China to Indonesia – the Carpathian Basin lies on its edge.

mid-ocean ridgessmaller

Earthquakes are frequent at the boundaries of diverging plates, but rarely large. The large, destructive earthquakes are all linked to the main belts.

02 · sizeMagnitude and intensity

An earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities. The magnitude (M) is a measure of the energy released at the focus – it was introduced by the American seismologist Richter. The scale is logarithmic: an increase of one unit means ten times the amplitude and about 32 times more energy. Intensity, on the other hand, is the strength of the effect at a given place, and it decreases with distance from the focus. The MSK-64 scale uses 12 degrees, in Roman numerals, based on the effects characteristic of the phenomena. In the early 1990s the European Seismological Commission built on its principles to develop the European Macroseismic Scale (EMS); EMS-98 determines the degree of intensity from three factors: the effect on people, on objects and on nature, and the damage to buildings.

Hungarian firsts

After the 1810 Mór earthquake, Pál Kitaibel and Ádám Tomcsányi produced in 1814 what is perhaps the world’s first isoseismal map. The relationship describing the decrease of intensity with distance was formulated by Radó Kövesligethy, the founder of Hungarian seismology – the map above also uses it.

03 · at homeEarthquakes in Hungary

Hungary is not considered highly exposed, but it is not earthquake-free either. The national seismological network records 100–150 smaller earthquakes a year, most of which cannot be felt; a few a year are clearly felt around the epicentre but do not yet cause damage. An earthquake causing more significant damage occurs every decade, and a strong one causing heavy damage (M 5.5–6.0) on average every 40–70 years. The first known earthquake in Hungary occurred on 7 September 456 in Savaria, near today’s Szombathely (M ≈ 6.1). The area stretching from Komárom to the northern end of Lake Balaton is still one of the most active parts of the country; in the east, near the Hungarian–Romanian border, the activity of the Érmellék seismic zone has been known since 1829 – the damage from the 1834 earthquake can still be seen in the church of Máriapócs.

04 · designDesign to EC8

The seismic design of a facility must be carried out according to EN 1998 (Eurocode 8), for the earthquake with a 10 % probability of exceedance in 50 years – this corresponds to a return period of about 475 years – as an accidental action. The seismicity of Hungary is shown by the ground acceleration map: the horizontal reference peak ground acceleration agR characteristic of a given area is between 0.08 and 0.15 g. Depending on the importance of the building, it is multiplied by the importance factor γI (0.8 minor, 1.0 ordinary, 1.2 large occupancy, 1.4 vital structures).

Ground types: the soil amplifies the earthquake

The same earthquake causes much stronger shaking on soft ground than on rock. EN 1998-1 therefore distinguishes seven ground types according to seismic behaviour. The classification is based on the upper 30 m below the structure. Three properties are examined: the shear wave velocity (vs,30), the SPT blow count (NSPT) and the undrained shear strength of the soil (cu). In Hungary most soils belong to ground types C and D.

05 · site effectsResonance and liquefaction – what the ground adds

The greatest earthquake damage often occurs not near the focus but where the ground amplifies the vibration or loses its bearing capacity. If the natural period of a thick, soft soil layer (T0 = 4·H/vs) coincides with the natural period of the buildings (approximately T ≈ 0.1·N s, where N is the number of storeys), resonance occurs. And in loose, saturated sand cyclic shearing increases the pore water pressure, the pressure between the grains disappears, and the soil behaves like a dense liquid: this is liquefaction.

CH3cellar · mine · tunnel

Underground cavities

There is a direct risk of settlement if an artificial cavity – a tunnel, a mine, a cellar – has to be excavated beneath existing surface structures, or if an old, forgotten cavity begins to fail. The cavity changes the original state of stress: the soil masses above and beside it move towards the cavity, and a settlement trough forms at the surface.

01 · mechanismWhat happens around the cavity?

Before the cavity is opened, the stresses in the ground are in equilibrium. Excavating the cavity removes this equilibrium: the load is redistributed around the cavity (arching), and the walls and the crown move towards the free space. If the cavity is not supported, or the support fails (for example when the tuff wall of a cellar becomes soaked), the soil squeezes into the cavity – and exactly this volume is “missing” at the surface too. The magnitude of settlement can therefore be estimated from the soil mass squeezed into the cavity.

The moving soil mass above the cavity is bounded by rupture surfaces: these run up from the lower corners of the cavity to the surface at an angle of about 45° + φ/2 to the horizontal, and they mark the width over which the surface settles. The deeper the cavity and the stronger the soil, the smaller and the later the displacement. It is important that the cavity is supported (or backfilled) and dewatered as soon as possible.

02 · cellar townsCellars under the towns

Many Hungarian towns – Eger, Pécs, Szekszárd, Szentendre, Ostoros, Velence, Nagymaros, Hőgyész, Budafok, Kőbánya – have extensive cellar systems beneath them. They are often of considerable size: there are also interconnected systems on two, three or four levels, with branches 50–300 m long. The old wine cellars and quarries were often dug before the towns spread over them, their records are incomplete, and the owners of the houses built above them often do not even know about them. The wall and vault of a cellar become dangerous when they get soaked: in Eger, for example, the strength of rhyodacite tuff drops to a third to a fifth when saturated with water. The economic impact of building damage in undermined areas is not negligible either.

03 · tunnels, minesTunnelling under the houses

Urban tunnelling is also cavity excavation: behind the shield tunnelling machine, because of the gap between the lining ring and the soil and the movement of the soil ahead of the face, a settlement trough travels along the tunnel alignment at the surface. The shape of the trough is well approximated by a Gaussian curve: the maximum settlement is above the tunnel axis, and the inflection point, where the trough is steepest, lies at a distance i = K·z0 from the axis. The volume of the trough can be calculated from the volume loss (a few tenths of a per cent of the tunnel cross-section) – for modern face-supported shields it is typically 0.3–0.5 %.

04 · protectionWhat to do if there is a cavity under the building?

01 · investigation

Find it

Archive maps, site walkover, boreholes, probing, ground-penetrating radar. An unknown cavity is the most dangerous.

02 · support

Support it

Masonry or reinforced concrete vault, shotcrete with mesh – as soon as possible, before movement starts.

03 · backfilling

Fill it

A redundant cavity is filled with fly-ash concrete, cement mortar or by grouting.

04 · water

Keep it dry

Rainwater and utility pipes must be routed away from the cavity: soaking is the main cause of failure.

CH4drawdown · grains · sump

Erosion by groundwater

Flowing water exerts not only pressure but also a force on the soil grains. If this force is large enough, it tears the finer grains out of the soil skeleton and carries them away: cavities form under the foundations and the building settles. Riverbanks, bridge piers, poorly dewatered excavations and the surroundings of leaking utility pipes are particularly at risk.

01 · rapid drawdownWhen the river falls faster than the groundwater

For structures built on granular soil on the banks of rivers, during rapid drawdown the groundwater beneath the bank cannot follow the river level fast enough: it stays higher in the ground. The level difference starts a strong flow towards the river, and this carries the grains away with it. Cavities form under the foundations. The same process is also very important for the foundations of river bridges. In Budapest it caused damage under the old Customs House – today the main building of Corvinus University.

02 · grainsWhen does a grain start to move?

Flowing water exerts a seepage force on the soil skeleton in the direction of flow, proportional to the hydraulic gradient (i = Δh/L, the fall in water level per unit length of flow path). For upward flow, at the critical gradient, icr = γ′/γw ≈ 1, the effective stress drops to zero: the soil loses its weight and behaves like a dense liquid – this is a boil, “quicksand”. A much smaller gradient is enough to wash the fine grains out of a loose skeleton (suffusion) – especially in gap-graded, uniform fine sand and silt.

03 · bridgesScour around the bridge pier

On the upstream side of a pier standing in a river, the water turns downwards, a vortex forms around the pier, and this digs a funnel-shaped scour hole in the riverbed. During a flood, the scour can reach below the pier foundation: the pier settles or tilts. Bridge foundations must therefore be taken below the expected scour depth, the bed must be protected with riprap, and the surroundings of the piers must be checked regularly after floods.

The pier of the bridge over the Douro settled because of scour caused by decades of riverbed dredging and the winter floods; the bridge fell into the river together with a bus and three cars.

04 · sumpQuicksand and sump pumping

Dewatering of basements and excavations is often done by pumping from a sump formed at the lowest point of the pit. If the structure is built on quicksand, the pump draws out the sand together with the water: the soil beneath the structure – and the neighbouring building – loosens and cavities form, which can lead to significant settlement. A warning sign is if the pumped water is cloudy and sandy. The solution: dewatering with filtered wells or a filter layer, and continuous monitoring of the sand content of the pumped water.

05 · utilitiesBurst pipe: washout beneath the road

Water escaping from a leaking water main or sewer carries away the soil in the same way – only below the surface, invisibly. The road or pavement surfacing bridges the growing cavity for a while, then suddenly collapses. Leaking utility pipes next to buildings can therefore also wash out cavities under the foundations; utilities must be connected to the building with flexible joints that tolerate movement, and leaks must be stopped as soon as possible.

CH5ice lens · frost depth · cold store

Frost action

Water freezing in the ground lifts the ground and the foundation on it – and on thawing the loosened, soaked soil settles back. Foundation depth, thermal insulation and heating protect against frost action. It is particularly dangerous if frost acts continuously over a long time: such is the soil beneath the floor of cold stores.

01 · mechanismIn-situ freezing and ice lenses

If the water in the soil simply freezes in place (in-situ freezing), its volume increases by about 9 % – compared with the volume of pore water this gives only a small heave. Large frost heave is caused by ice lenses: water flows from the still unfrozen soil below the frost front through the capillaries towards the frozen zone, where it freezes into horizontal ice layers one above another. The ice lenses thicken in the direction of heat flow, and their total thickness can be measured in centimetres, in severe cases in decimetres.

Frost heave requires three conditions together: frost-susceptible (fine-grained) soil, frost penetrating the ground, and a water supply. The most susceptible are silts and very fine, silty sands: they are fine enough for water to rise by capillarity, but permeable enough for it to be replenished quickly. Gravel is not frost-susceptible, and high-plasticity clay conducts water so slowly that ice lenses grow slowly.

A related phenomenon: swelling and shrinking clay

Like frost action, the water content of the soil also lifts and lowers the building on expansive clays – but here no ice forms. High-plasticity clay swells when it takes up water and shrinks when it dries: in winter and spring it lifts a lightly loaded house, and by late summer and autumn it lowers it again; the cracks open and close with the seasons. This effect is covered in detail on another page.

02 · frost depthThe foundation must go below the frost depth

One consideration in setting the foundation level is that the footing must be below the frost depth. In Hungarian practice the frost depth is 0.8 m: a smaller depth (0.5 m) is acceptable only for sand and gravel if the groundwater is deeper than 2 m; for ground levels above 300 m above sea level the frost depth is 0.8 or 1.0 m. The depth of frost penetration depends on the severity and duration of the frost (the freezing index), on the thermal conductivity and on the water content of the soil: in moist soil the latent heat of the freezing water slows the frost front down.

03 · buildingsWhere does the ground freeze under a building?

In normal, heated buildings it is mainly the foundation of the external walls and the basement floor that are exposed to frost. Next to the external wall, the intermittent melting of accumulated snow keeps the soil moist, and if the foundation is above the frost depth, the frost penetrates below it too. If the basement window is left open in winter, the air in the basement cools down and the soil below the floor freezes as well: the floor heaves and the walls crack. The same threatens half-finished buildings left unheated in winter and open excavations, where the already excavated foundation level can freeze.

04 · cold storeThe soil beneath a cold store

After a cold store is put into service, the “zero isotherm” – the surface of points at 0 °C – penetrates deeper until the heat loss of the soil is made up by the heat flowing from the Earth’s interior. With poor insulation the soil below the floor can freeze to a depth of 6–12 m, and over the years this causes heave of the floor and the building. To prevent frost damage, a thermal insulation layer must be built in (formerly e.g. bituminous cork, today extruded polystyrene), and instead of or together with the insulation, underfloor heating – heating pipes, heating cables or ventilation ducts below the insulation – may also be used to keep the soil below the floor above freezing.

A floor that humps up over the years

Floor heaves of 15 and 41 cm were measured in two refrigerated buildings. The cause was frost heave of the natural soil 2–4 m below the floor, as the frost slowly penetrated deeper year by year.

a few monthsa cold store

The underfloor heating failed

The soil beneath the floor of a cold store was kept above freezing by electric heating. The heating, which had been added later, failed after a few months, and severe frost damage occurred. Underfloor heating is therefore not a “fit and forget” solution: it has to be operated and checked.

Today’s solution in brief

Cold store floors today typically have 10–15 cm of extruded polystyrene insulation, below which glycol heating pipes or heating cables placed every 60–120 cm keep the soil a few degrees above freezing. The heating power required is small, about 10–20 W/m² – much cheaper than the damage caused by frost heave.
